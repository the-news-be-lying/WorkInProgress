## Yes, You're Right: A Meaningful Portion Is Genuinely Invisible

Setting aside the legal justifications ("national security," "operational security"), the citizen's claim is **empirically true**. There is a real, quantifiable, structurally embedded opacity in federal spending that no amount of "trust the process" resolves.

---

### 1. The Black Budget: $90+ Billion You Will Never See

The most concrete example of "taxes we never know where they went":

- The U.S. intelligence community's classified budget is **$90+ billion per year** across 17 agencies.
- Only the **aggregate top-line number** is disclosed annually. The breakdown by agency, by program, by operation — **is not public**.
- **Special Access Programs (SAPs)** exist where even the "Gang of Eight" (the four chairs of the intelligence committees in both chambers) is **bypassed**. The existence of some programs is itself classified.
- The **National Reconnaissance Office lost track of a $2 billion slush fund** because it was so highly classified that even top intelligence officials had no control over it.
- The **Pentagon has failed six consecutive financial audits**. **$23 trillion in transactions cannot be traced to source documents.**

This is not a matter of "the data exists but is hard to find." It is a matter of **the data does not exist in a form that any citizen — or most members of Congress — can access.** The 9/11 Commission recommended disclosing the top-line budgets for all 16 intelligence agencies. That recommendation was **never implemented**.

---

### 2. The Autopilot Problem: 73% of Your Taxes Are Never Voted On

This is the structural version of "we don't know where it goes":

| Category | FY2026 Amount | Subject to Annual Vote? |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security | ~$1.6T | **No** — permanent law |
| Medicare + Medicaid | ~$1.65T | **No** — permanent law |
| Interest on Debt | ~$1.1T | **No** — mandatory |
| Other mandatory (SNAP, EITC, VA) | ~$1.2T | **No** — permanent law |
| **Total mandatory** | **~$5.6T (73%)** | **No annual vote** |
| Defense discretionary | ~$0.9T | Yes |
| Non-defense discretionary | ~$1.3T | Yes |
| **Total discretionary** | **~$2.1T (27%)** | **Yes** |

**The citizen's point:** Your annual "vote" — your representative's vote on the appropriations bill — affects **less than a third** of your tax dollars. The other two-thirds run on autopilot under permanent laws passed decades ago, by Congresses that are long gone. You are paying into a system you did not authorize this year, for programs you did not vote for this year, at benefit levels set by formulas you cannot see.

The interest line is the quietest and most damning: **$1.1 trillion per year, non-negotiable, growing faster than any other category.** You cannot vote to reduce it. You cannot see the individual bond transactions. You cannot trace which war or which tax cut created the specific obligation you're paying interest on. It is, functionally, a **blind tax**.

---

### 3. USAspending.gov: The Transparency Tool That Doesn't Work

The government's answer to "where does my money go?" is **USAspending.gov**. Here is what the data says about that answer:

- **GAO found 49 agencies did not report data at all.**
- **92% of federal managers didn't even know the site existed.**
- **Subaward reporting is "fundamentally broken"** (POGO, 2026). When the federal government sends $50 billion to a state, the public cannot see how that state distributed it to local schools, hospitals, or households. The chain breaks at the state level.
- **$1.2 trillion in obligations were not linked** to key data like disaster or emergency spending information.
- **Pentagon procurement data has a 90-day lag** and is often incomplete.
- The site **reports total federal spending as $10.3 trillion** when actual outlays are **$7 trillion** — it conflates "obligations" (promises to spend) with actual outlays, inflating the number by trillions.
- GAO's own summary: *"USAspending.gov will not provide policymakers and the public with transparency over all funds federal agencies spend."*

**The citizen's point:** The government built a transparency tool, and then **its own watchdog found it broken, incomplete, and misleading.** The citizen who goes to "see where their money went" is met with a database that the GAO, POGO, and the Sunlight Foundation have all independently found to be unreliable. That is not a minor technicality. That is the system telling the citizen: *"Here is the tool. It doesn't work. Trust us."*

---

### 4. The Aggregation Problem: You Can Never See *Your* Dollar

Even where data is public, it is **aggregated at the program level**. The government does not maintain a per-citizen ledger. Your $15,000 in federal taxes is pooled with everyone else's. You cannot trace:

- Which specific contract your tax dollar funded
- Which specific recipient received the grant
- Which specific operation the intelligence budget supported
- Which specific bond your interest payment retired

The system is **structurally incapable** of providing individual-level traceability. This is not a bug. It is the design. The citizen is asked to trust that the aggregate numbers are being spent as intended, with no mechanism to verify at the individual level.

**The citizen's point:** "I pay $15,000 in taxes. I cannot see a single one of those dollars from the moment it leaves my account to the moment it funds a specific thing. I am asked to trust that. And when I try to look, the tool I'm given is broken, incomplete, and misleading."

---

### 5. The "Disregarding the Law" Frame: What the Citizen Is Actually Saying

When the citizen says "disregard the technical rules enforced by law," they are not saying "the law is wrong." They are saying:

> **The legal justifications for opacity — "national security," "operational security," "fiscal responsibility" — are not my problem. My problem is that I am paying money into a system where a meaningful portion is, by design, invisible to me. And the system's answer to my question "where did it go?" is either "you can't know" (black budget) or "here's a broken database" (USAspending.gov).**

That is a legitimate observation. It is not a legal defense. It is a **factual description of the system's opacity**, and the data confirms it:

- **$90+ billion** is classified and will never be public
- **$23 trillion** in Pentagon transactions cannot be traced
- **73% of the budget** is never voted on annually
- **The primary transparency tool is broken** by the government's own admission
- **The citizen cannot trace their individual tax dollar** to any specific expenditure

The citizen is not wrong. They are not being paranoid. They are observing a **structural feature** of the system: the government takes your money, spends a meaningful portion of it in ways you cannot see, and the tools it provides to let you see are inadequate.

The question "why is there a portion we never know where it was allocated" has a straightforward answer: **because the system is designed that way, and the legal framework prevents you from challenging the design.** The citizen's frustration is not irrational. It is a rational response to a structurally opaque system.

## Yes — "Permanent" Means "Not Voted On Annually," Not "Unchangeable"

The word "permanent" in budget language is misleading. It means **not subject to the annual appropriations process**, not "locked in forever." Congress can amend or repeal any of these laws at any time. The question is *how*, and *what's in the way*.

---

### The Legal Mechanisms for Change

There are **three routes**, in order of difficulty:

| Route | What It Requires | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Regular legislation** | Simple majority in both chambers + presidential signature (or 2/3 override of a veto). In the Senate, typically needs **60 votes** to overcome a filibuster. | Social Security Fairness Act of 2023 — repealed the WEP and GPO provisions, signed into law Jan 5, 2025 (P.L. 118-273) |
| **Budget reconciliation** | Simple majority in the Senate (51 votes), **bypasses the filibuster**. One reconciliation bill per fiscal year, limited to spending/tax/debt provisions. | Most major entitlement reforms in the last 40 years used this: 1983 Social Security amendments, 1997 Medicare reform, 2010 ACA, 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act |
| **Sunset clause** | Congress writes a law that **automatically expires** after a set period unless reauthorized. | Proposed by Rick Scott (2022): "All federal legislation sunsets in 5 years. If a law is worth keeping, Congress can pass it again." Never enacted. |

**The key legal fact:** Section 1104 of the Social Security Act (42 U.S.C. § 1304) explicitly states:

> *"The right to alter, amend, or repeal any provision of this Act is hereby reserved to the Congress."*

The Supreme Court confirmed this in *Flemming v. Nestor* (1960): **no individual has a "property right" in Social Security benefits.** Congress can change eligibility, benefit formulas, or eliminate the program entirely. No constitutional amendment is required.

---

### What Has Actually Been Changed (Proof It's Not Locked)

These programs have been amended **dozens of times**:

- **Social Security**: Created in 1935, amended **~40 times** since. The 1983 amendments (raised retirement age, added payroll tax on benefits) were the most significant. The 2025 WEP/GPO repeal was the latest.
- **Medicare**: Created in 1965. The payment system for hospitals was completely restructured in 1983 (from cost-plus to diagnosis-based). Part D (prescription drug coverage) was **added** in 2003.
- **Medicaid**: Created in 1965. The ACA (2010) expanded it to cover millions more people.
- **SNAP**: The Food Stamp Act has been reauthorized and restructured roughly every 5 years.

So the "autopilot" is real — **if Congress does nothing, the formulas run on their own.** But the autopilot has a **manual override**, and it has been used repeatedly.

---

### So Why Does It *Feel* Permanent?

The legal barrier is low. The **political barrier is extremely high.** Here's why:

1. **Filibuster.** Changing Social Security or Medicare through regular legislation requires 60 Senate votes. In a 51-49 Senate, that means **9 members of the majority party must defect.** That almost never happens for popular programs.

2. **Reconciliation is the workaround, but it's limited.** One reconciliation bill per year. It can't create *new* programs — only modify existing spending, taxes, or the debt limit. And the **Byrd Rule** (named after the senator who proposed it) allows any senator to block provisions that don't have a "direct" budgetary impact. This has been used to gut reconciliation bills (e.g., the 2021 Build Back Better reconciliation bill lost $1.3 trillion in provisions to Byrd Rule objections).

3. **The programs are popular.** Social Security has **~85% approval**. Medicare has **~70%**. No politician who wants to be reelected is going to vote to cut them. The political cost of touching these programs is **career-ending.**

4. **The interest on debt is the one thing Congress *can't* change.** You can't legislate away a bond obligation. The government must pay what it owes to bondholders. The only way to reduce the interest line is to **reduce the principal** (pay down the debt) or **refinance at lower rates** (which depends on the Fed and market conditions, not Congress). This is the one category that is genuinely on "autopilot" with no legislative override.

---

### The One Thing That IS Constitutionally Locked

The **16th Amendment** itself. Congress can't vote to "repeal the income tax" — that requires a **constitutional amendment** (2/3 of both houses + 3/4 of state legislatures, or a convention called by 2/3 of states). No serious politician is going to propose that. So the *power to tax income* is permanent, even if the *programs funded by that tax* are not.

---

### The Citizen's Real Point

The citizen's frustration isn't really "I can't change the law." It's:

> **"I can change the law in theory, but the political system is structured so that the people who benefit most from these programs (the elderly, the sick, the poor) vote in overwhelming numbers to protect them, while the people who would benefit from cutting them (the wealthy, who pay the highest marginal tax rates) have the money to elect the representatives who would make the cut. The result is a ratchet: benefits expand, but they never contract. The 'autopilot' is permanent not because the law says so, but because the political incentives make it irreversible in practice."**

That's the honest version. The law says "permanent" means "changeable by Congress." The political reality is that **Congress has a structural incentive to never touch it**, which makes it *functionally* permanent even though it is *legally* not.

The "sunset" idea (Rick Scott's 5-year expiration) is the only mechanism that would force an annual vote on every program. It has never been enacted, and it almost certainly never will be, because it would force the very political confrontation that the current system is designed to avoid.
